The company a word keeps

Two Word2Vec models, skip-gram and CBOW, each trained on the 118,250 words of Jane Austen’s Pride and Prejudice. Neither model was told what any word means. Everything here comes from which words appear near each other.

          The map

          Each word is a point in 100 dimensions. PCA flattens them to two so we can look. The heatmap keeps all 100 dimensions.

          Distances on a 2-D map can mislead: two words drawn close together may be far apart in the full space. Trust the heatmap for exact scores.

          About this model

          Text
          Pride and Prejudice by Jane Austen, Project Gutenberg #1342, license and preface removed
          Size
          2,215 paragraphs, 118,250 words, words in the vocabulary
          Settings
          Models
          Skip-gram (sg=1) and CBOW (sg=0), identical in every other setting

          Trained with Gensim in Google Colab. Each paragraph was joined onto one line, lowercased, and stripped of punctuation before training, so single-letter words like “a” and “i” aren’t in the vocabulary. Vectors are stored at reduced precision to keep this page small; the rankings match the original models.

          What the two models show: skip-gram links a word to the events and talk around it (Wickham sits beside account, cruelty, and history), while CBOW groups words that play the same role (Darcy beside Bingley, Collins, and Wickham). With a single short novel, CBOW’s averaging smooths out the noise, which is why it solves the Jane : Bingley :: Elizabeth : Darcy analogy and skip-gram doesn’t.
